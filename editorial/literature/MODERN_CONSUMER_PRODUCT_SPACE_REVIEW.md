# Modern Consumer Theory for Very Large Product Spaces

Literature and modeling review for the Fall 2026 edition
Prepared: 19 September 2026

## Editorial recommendation

The end of the consumer side should not introduce one catch-all “continuum of goods” model. It should teach students to choose among three distinct economic objects:

| Economic question | Choice object | Demand object | Appropriate framework |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which single product is bought? | One alternative in a product space | Choice cell or market share | Ideal-point, pure-characteristics, or random-utility demand |
| Which products and quantities form a basket? | A positive measure over product space | Measure-valued Marshallian or Hicksian demand | Infinite-dimensional consumer theory or multiple-discrete/continuous demand |
| Who receives a capacity-constrained product or quality? | A joint assignment of consumer and product types | Coupling with fixed marginals | Hedonic matching or optimal transport |

The distinction matters. A large catalog does not itself imply local substitution. A metric does not itself imply that close products are substitutes. Independent choice from an unlimited-replication catalog is not yet a matching market. Each conclusion needs its own assumptions.

The recommended textbook section is **“The Many-Product Consumer.”** Its internal spine is product space, neighborhood demand, bundles, and matching. It should synthesize the preceding utility, revealed-preference, stochastic-choice, attention, and aggregation chapters rather than create a disconnected new theory.

## 1. Scale, attention, and the relevant choice set

The motivating scale is real, although the proposed “one billion Amazon products” statistic is not verified. Amazon currently describes its store as carrying **more than 300 million products across more than 35 categories**, and elsewhere as carrying hundreds of millions of products. The book should use that dated, documented scale and note that the exact catalog count changes. See [Amazon Stores](https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do/amazon-store).

At that scale, three sets must be separated:

1. the **available catalog**;
2. the **consideration or exposure set** generated by search, ranking, advertising, and recommendation;
3. the **purchased support** receiving positive quantity.

This sequence is economically more useful than treating the posted catalog as the consumer’s literal menu:

\[
\text{availability}\longrightarrow\text{exposure/consideration}
\longrightarrow\text{choice}\longrightarrow\text{basket}.
\]

[Abaluck and Adams-Prassl (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab008) show why consideration cannot generally be read directly from purchases. [Gandhi, Lu, and Shi (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE1593) show why zero empirical market shares are endemic in granular differentiated-product data and cannot safely be repaired by dropping or replacing zeros.

## 2. One-item demand in product space

Let products be locations \(g_j\) in a compact metric characteristic space \((\mathcal G,d)\), and let consumer \(i\) have an ideal point \(b_i\). A transparent baseline is

\[
U^*_{ij}=\delta_j-p_j-\kappa_d d(b_i,g_j)^2.
\]

Demand for product \(j\) is the mass of its generalized weighted Voronoi cell. Equal prices and quality intercepts imply nearest-product choice. With unequal prices or qualities, the chosen product need not be nearest.

For this baseline, choice is conditional on purchase: every consumer selects exactly one item, all products are considered, and the ideal-point distribution is a probability measure. An outside option can be added to study participation, but then some demand lost after a price increase may leave the market rather than divert to a neighboring product. Tie sets should be null or governed by an explicit tie-breaking rule.

This framework connects [Hotelling (1929)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2224214), [Lancaster (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1086/259131), and [Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse (1989)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297747). It also clarifies the relation to modern differentiated-product estimation:

- [Berry, Levinsohn, and Pakes (1995)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4264) use random coefficients so diversion depends on overlap in heterogeneous tastes and characteristics.
- [Berry and Pakes (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2354.2007.00459.x) remove the iid product shock in a pure-characteristics model, making product geometry more central.
- [Feenstra and Levinsohn (1995)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297840) model multidimensional product attributes and an endogenous local competitor structure.
- [Pinkse, Slade, and Brett (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00320) estimate cross-price responses flexibly enough to distinguish global from neighbor-only competition.

### A tractable local-diversion result

In one dimension with the Euclidean metric \(d(b,g)=|b-g|\), ordered active products, and quadratic mismatch cost, the indifference boundary between adjacent products is

\[
\bar b_{j,j+1}
=\frac{g_j+g_{j+1}}2
+\frac{\delta_j-\delta_{j+1}-p_j+p_{j+1}}
{2\kappa_d(g_{j+1}-g_j)}.
\]

If product \(j\)'s active neighbors remain \(j-1\) and \(j+1\), then

\[
s_j=F(\bar b_{j,j+1})-F(\bar b_{j-1,j}).
\]

Only the two active neighbors have nonzero direct local cross-price effects. An increase in \(p_j\) raises only the shares of \(j-1\) and \(j+1\), and those gains sum to the loss in \(s_j\). This conservation statement uses the absence of an active outside option. The result is local: adjacency must be stable on an open price neighborhood, inactive alternatives must be strictly slack at the boundaries, ties must be negligible, and the utility specification—not the metric alone—creates the boundary movement.

Conditional simple logit without an outside option provides the contrast. For \(j\ne k\),

\[
\frac{\partial s_j}{\partial p_k}=\alpha_p s_js_k>0,
\]

so every product substitutes for every other product regardless of distance. Random coefficients enrich diversion, but distance decay remains an economic restriction to estimate or test.

## 3. Bundles over a continuum of goods

For shopping baskets, the commodity should be a finite nonnegative Radon measure \(x\in\mathcal M_+(\mathcal G)\). A continuous strictly positive price schedule \(p\in C_{++}(\mathcal G)\) evaluates the basket through

\[
\langle p,x\rangle=\int_{\mathcal G}p(g)x(dg).
\]

This representation includes finite observed baskets exactly:

\[
x=\sum_{j=1}^J q_j\delta_{g_j},
\qquad
\langle p,x\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^Jp(g_j)q_j.
\]

It also permits diffuse quantity densities when those are economically meaningful. An \(L^1\) density alone would exclude literal finite baskets on an atomless reference space; the measure formulation does not. [Mas-Colell (1975)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4068(75)90028-2) is the foundational differentiated-commodity reference, while [Bewley (1972)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(72)90136-6) emphasizes that topology and the price dual cannot be ignored in infinite-dimensional equilibrium theory.

### Dimension-free finite-data Afriat theorem

For finite observations \((p^t,x^t)_{t=1}^T\), all revealed-preference comparisons depend only on the finite matrix

\[
\langle p^t,x^s\rangle.
\]

Equip \(\mathcal M_+(\mathcal G)\subset C(\mathcal G)^*\) with the weak-star topology. For the through-observation budgets, finite-data GARP holds if and only if the finite expenditure matrix admits positive Afriat multipliers. For any such certificate,

\[
U(x)=\min_t\left\{U^t+\lambda^t
\langle p^t,x-x^t\rangle\right\}
\]

is a concave rationalization. On compact \(\mathcal G\), continuous strictly positive prices give more:

- each affine price functional is weak-star continuous;
- the finite minimum is weak-star continuous;
- the envelope is strictly increasing in the measure order;
- every future strictly positive continuous-price budget is weak-star compact, so demand is attained.

This is a direct corollary of the finite Afriat certificate plus Riesz duality and Banach–Alaoglu. It should be presented as a derived textbook proposition, not attributed as a verbatim theorem to an existing paper. It is also an excellent future Lean target: the algebraic envelope can first be generalized from coordinate vectors to an ordered vector space paired with positive linear functionals; weak-star compactness can remain a separate analytic theorem.

The local repository audit found finite-dimensional Afriat and WGARP formalizations, but no existing Victor Aguiar theorem specifically for a continuum of commodity labels. The book should therefore not describe this extension as previously formalized work without a more precise source pointer.

## 4. Neighborhood cross-price elasticities

For a dense product space, a \(J\times J\) elasticity matrix is neither stable across product entry nor easy to interpret. Let

\[
B_r(g)=\{g':d(g,g')\le r\}
\]

be a ball of nearby products. If Hicksian demand is a locally unique measure \(h\), fix closed regions \(B\) and continuous profiles satisfying \(0\leq\varphi_B\leq1\), \(\varphi_B=1\) on \(B\), and \(\operatorname{supp}\varphi_B\subseteq B^\rho\) for a common bandwidth \(\rho\). Set

\[
p_\varepsilon(g)=p(g)e^{\varepsilon\varphi_B(g)}.
\]

For a measurable region \(A\) with positive demand, assume directly that the scalar path \(\varepsilon\mapsto h(A;p_\varepsilon,\bar u)\) is differentiable at zero. The set-to-set compensated elasticity is

\[
\mathcal E^H(A;\varphi_B)
=\left.
\frac{d\log h(A;p_\varepsilon,\bar u)}{d\varepsilon}
\right|_{\varepsilon=0}.
\]

This object asks how total compensated demand in product cloud \(A\) responds to a standardized percentage price perturbation around cloud \(B\). On a finite catalog with the discrete topology, the profile can be an indicator. When prices must be continuous, an isolated point shock is generally inadmissible; with diffuse demand, a singleton also has zero mass.

Important boundaries:

- locality is an additional restriction, such as a nonincreasing bound on \(|\mathcal E^H(A;\varphi_B)|\) in the separation \(d(A,\operatorname{supp}\varphi_B)\) for standardized regions and profiles, not a consequence of rationality;
- bundles may contain complements, so neighborhood cross effects need not be positive;
- compensated and Marshallian effects can have different signs;
- a Fréchet derivative is an operator, and not every bounded operator admits a pointwise kernel;
- a learned embedding is predictive geometry, not automatically a causal cross-price elasticity.

## 5. When “matching” is the right word

If products are freely replicated at posted prices, consumers make independent choices. Their cells look like an assignment, but there is no market-level matching constraint.

Matching becomes exact when product types or sellers have capacity. Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be probability measures on compact consumer-type and product spaces \(\mathcal B\) and \(\mathcal G\), let \(S\) be continuous transferable surplus, and define \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\{\pi:\pi_1=\mu,\pi_2=\nu\}\). The assignment problem is

\[
\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\int_{\mathcal B\times\mathcal G} S(b,g)\,d\pi(b,g).
\]

[Chiappori, McCann, and Nesheim (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-009-0455-z) establish the relation among quasilinear hedonic equilibrium, stable transferable-utility matching, and optimal transport. Compactness and continuity give existence for the displayed planner problem. The fixed second marginal exhausts supply; upper capacities or unmatched agents require marginal inequalities, partial transport, or dummy types. A hedonic-market interpretation additionally requires participation conditions and the dual potentials supporting the assignment as utilities and prices. On a Euclidean type space, a pure match generally requires an absolutely continuous source distribution plus twist and further regularity; otherwise the optimal coupling need not be generated by one matching function.

This is different from the later deferred-acceptance chapter, where both sides have ordinal preferences and stability rather than transferable-surplus optimality is the central concept.

## 6. Audit of Neiman and Vavra

[Neiman and Vavra (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1257/mac.20210263) is the right empirical bridge into the section.

### What it establishes

- Nielsen Homescan, 2004–2016, roughly 170,000 households, more than 22,000 ZIP codes, and almost 700 million transactions.
- Within Nielsen product groups, each household's annual UPC-spending HHI rises while aggregate UPC-spending HHI falls.
- Households buy narrower baskets but increasingly disagree about which UPCs belong in those baskets.
- Product entry and exit are central to the pattern.
- The model combines CES consumption inside an endogenous subset with the utility cost \(F|\Omega_i|^\epsilon\) of expanding that subset; this is not a monetary access cost in the budget constraint.
- A price response contains an intensive margin and an extensive customer-entry margin; niche-product demand is more elastic.
- The model infers roughly 70 percent growth in effective variety during 2004–2016. In the \(N\)-only counterfactual, which holds \(\alpha\) fixed and assigns that growth to catalog variety, welfare rises about 0.56 percent per year in covered categories; improved selection contributes 0.52 percentage points, or about 93 percent of that gain.
- The \(N\)-only counterfactual does not reproduce the observed decline in \(|\Omega|\). Specifications matching all three targeted trends imply annual welfare gains of 0.45–0.47 percent.

### What it does not establish

- Its products have common and idiosyncratic ranks, not locations in a metric characteristic space.
- Adjacent ranks need not be similar goods, so the model does not produce neighborhood cross-price elasticities.
- Household HHI, aggregate HHI, and the observed average number of consumed varieties \(|\Omega|\)—not the two concentration moments alone—identify the dispersion parameter \(\eta\) and effective variety \(\widetilde N=N\alpha/(1-\alpha)\). They do not identify catalog size \(N\) separately from taste disagreement \(\alpha\).
- The welfare numbers are calibrated structural implications, not direct treatment effects.
- The evidence covers grocery and household nondurables, not an Amazon-wide online catalog.
- The model does not separately represent search, recommendation, or consideration sets.

The clean textbook use is therefore: Neiman–Vavra motivates scale, selection, heterogeneous baskets, and extensive-margin elasticities; ideal-point models supply metric nearness; the attention chapter supplies consideration; matching enters only with capacity.

## 7. Literature map by question

### Product geometry and local competition

- [Hotelling (1929)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2224214): spatial/ideal-point origin.
- [Lancaster (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1086/259131): utility over characteristics rather than product names.
- [Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse (1989)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297747): explicit bridge among characteristics, differentiated-product demand systems, and discrete choice.
- [Berry and Pakes (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2354.2007.00459.x): pure-characteristics demand.
- [Feenstra and Levinsohn (1995)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297840): multidimensional ideal points and local competitors.
- [Pinkse, Slade, and Brett (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00320): empirical test of local versus global competition.

### Variety, baskets, and welfare

- [Brynjolfsson, Hu, and Smith (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.49.11.1580.20580): online variety and consumer surplus.
- [Brynjolfsson, Hu, and Simester (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1371): search costs and the long tail.
- [Broda and Weinstein (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.3.691): product turnover and cost-of-living measurement.
- [Li (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_00879): household variety, expenditure, and access costs.
- [Neiman and Vavra (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1257/mac.20210263): niche consumption, selection, and household disagreement.

### Attention, sparse shares, and large catalogs

- [Abaluck and Adams-Prassl (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjab008): identify consideration from asymmetric demand responses.
- [Gandhi, Lu, and Shi (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE1593): differentiated-product estimation with zero empirical shares.

### Infinite-dimensional demand and matching

- [Mas-Colell (1975)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4068(75)90028-2): differentiated commodities as measures over characteristic space.
- [Bewley (1972)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(72)90136-6): equilibrium with infinitely many commodities and the role of topology/duality.
- [Chiappori, McCann, and Nesheim (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-009-0455-z): hedonic equilibrium, transferable-utility matching, and optimal transport.

## 8. Dependency and verification record

The accompanying Lean narrative map checks four structural claims about the section:

1. every declared dependency precedes the claim using it;
2. identifiers are unique and ordered;
3. the empirical finding depends on an empirical input;
4. analytic results and model assumptions do not depend on later empirical evidence.

That check does **not** verify the empirical estimates, the prose, the unformalized local-diversion calculus, or the functional-analytic Afriat proposition. Those remain separately auditable mathematical and empirical claims.

## 9. Claims the textbook should avoid

- “Amazon has one billion products.” Use the documented hundreds-of-millions scale unless a dated source is supplied.
- “A continuum of goods implies nearby substitution.” It does not.
- “Logit captures product nearness.” Simple logit has global proportional substitution.
- “Choosing the closest product is always optimal.” Only under equal price and quality terms in the stated ideal-point model.
- “Consumer–product choice is matching.” Not without capacity or two-sided feasibility constraints.
- “The Neiman–Vavra model is a spatial model.” Its heterogeneity is rank-based, not metric.
- “Afriat automatically solves infinite-dimensional theory.” The finite certificate extends cleanly under a dual pairing, but continuity, compactness, equilibrium, and differentiation require topology and regularity.
